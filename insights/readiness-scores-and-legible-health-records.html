Insights · Health data

Readiness scores are here. A health record's job is to stay legible.

On 9 September, Apple announced watches that read heart rate every five seconds and heart-rate variability as often as every five minutes, and a redesigned Health app that will summarize it all with Apple Intelligence. That is a lot more signal. It changes what a personal health record is for.

Published 19 September 2026 · 5 min read · By Arctura Technologies

What Apple announced, in brief

Apple Watch Series 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4 carry what Apple calls a Health Sensing System: new optical and electrical heart sensors that measure heart rate every five seconds all day, heart-rate variability as often as every five minutes (Apple's figure is 24 times more often than before), a new readiness score, and a Vitals view that now includes HRV compared against a personal baseline. A redesigned Health app is due on iPhone later this year with a Longevity tab, a Health Age feature, and health insights generated by Apple Intelligence. Apple frames the whole announcement around "keeping their personal health data private, secure, and under their control." The watches reached stores on 18 September.

We build Linnea, a personal health record and score app that reads Apple Health on iPhone, so we read announcements like this closely — not as competition (the Health app is the ground truth for what the phone knows) but as a change in the raw material. Here is what we think more signal means for a health record, and how Linnea is built for it.

1. Aggregate on the device. Move as little as possible.

Five-second heart rate is among the most sensitive forms of consumer health data and, for everyday decisions, largely unnecessary in raw form. Nobody needs roughly 17,000 heart-rate samples a day copied to a server to learn that they slept badly. Linnea's rule is to read Apple Health on the device, reduce it to daily aggregates — sleep totals, steps, workout minutes, active energy, mindful minutes, weight trend — and let only those aggregates leave the phone. Raw Apple Health samples never do. Our service rejects payloads shaped like raw samples, so the rule is enforced on the server rather than promised in a document. The score itself is computed on the device as well.

The more precise wearables become, the more this matters. The risk in health software is rarely that data is stored; it is that more is stored than the job requires.

2. A score has to show its work

A readiness score, a Today score, a health age: each compresses dozens of inputs into one number. The number is convenient. It is only useful if you can see what moved it. Linnea composes one 0–100 Today score from five sub-signals, and tapping any tile shows exactly what moved it. Trends and baselines are built from your own history across weeks and years — the same instinct behind Apple's decision to analyze recovery HRV against a personal baseline rather than a fixed threshold. And when Linnea says something unprompted, it is one observational sentence ("Noticed"), and only when a real pattern shows up in your own data.

Restraint is a feature here. A record that speaks constantly trains you to ignore it, and a score that cannot explain itself is a mood ring with decimals.

3. Claim less than the data allows

Higher-frequency data invites bigger claims. We go the other way. Every AI-assisted result in Linnea carries a versioned notice stating its limits; the Today score's notice reads: "Today score is a behavioral wellness observation, not medical guidance. Discuss persistent changes with your clinician." Linnea is not a medical device, does not provide medical advice, and never suggests medication changes. That is not legal caution added at the end; it is the design brief. Information tools help people notice and organize. Clinicians decide.

What the record adds that the sensor cannot

Wearables see the body's signals. They do not see the meal, the medication, the supplement, the glass of water, or the mood you would describe in a sentence. A personal health record earns its place by holding both halves — the sensed and the stated — on one timeline, for years, in a form you can export (Linnea exports everything as JSON) and delete. As the sensed half gets richer, the stated half becomes the part nobody else has.

The bar just moved

Better sensors are good news, and they raise the bar for everyone who builds on top of them. Our test for any health-record feature now has three questions. Does it move less data than it could? Does it explain itself? Does it claim less than it could? That is the standard we hold ourselves to, and the standard we think users should hold any health app to — including ours.
