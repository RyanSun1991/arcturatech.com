Siri AI is opening to every app. "On your device" now has three meanings, and a health app should say which one it means.

Two Apple changes this autumn pull in opposite directions, and both are good. iOS 27.2 adds Excluded Apps, one setting where a person decides which apps Siri, Search and suggestions can reach. On 3 November Apple opened a developer preview that lets any app bring its content and actions into Siri AI through App Intents. Between them, the phrase "on your device" is about to cover more ground than it used to. Here are three things it can mean, which one Linnea means for each feature, and the sentence our own privacy policy does not yet contain.

Published 4 November 2026 · 5 min read · By Arctura Technologies

Meaning one: it stays in the app

The first meaning is the plain one. The work happens on the phone, inside the app, and nothing leaves. Linnea's privacy policy and its App Store listing use it only where it is literally true. A photo of a nutrition label is read on your device and is not uploaded. A paper lab report is scanned and every value is recognized on your device, without a connection, and you check each value before it is saved. Raw Apple Health and Health Connect samples stay on your device; our servers keep daily totals, your workouts and nights of sleep. Since the 2.8.3 update, released on 3 November, the photo you log a meal with is kept only on the device you logged it on, encrypted, and deleted with the entry, so other devices and family members see a dish icon instead, and meal photo previews are no longer included in iPhone and iPad backups. Each of those is a sentence about one step of one feature: where a lab report is read, not where the encrypted record is then stored; where a meal photo is kept, not where it is recognized. None of them is a sentence about the app.

Meaning two: it stays on the phone, and the system can read it

The second meaning is newer. Data can sit on the phone, never touching a server of ours, and still be read by the operating system's assistant. Apple's iPhone User Guide describes the arrangement: an app that is open to Siri can be searched and acted on by the assistant, which may also offer suggestions before being asked; every app starts out included; adding one to Excluded Apps takes it out of reach of Siri, Search and suggestions alike. The 3 November developer preview widens what an app can offer. With App Intents, an app's content and actions become something Siri AI can use, with personal context and on-screen awareness, and in Apple's words, "Siri AI can now surface any entity in the Spotlight semantic index". Apple says the preview reaches customers in a few months, App Store submissions open in the spring, and the terms and usage policies for these experiences are still to come.

For a health app, that turns a design decision into a privacy statement. What an app donates to the system index is what the assistant can find, and it does not matter that none of it crossed the internet. What Linnea publishes about Siri today is all on the input side of that line. You can log medications, water, mood and more by asking Siri; the policy records where each entry was made, lists Siri among the services Apple provides under its own terms, and says our notifications carry only fixed text or a signal to update, never your health information. What the policy does not say is what, if anything, Linnea makes available for Siri, Search or suggestions to find. Until it does, the honest answer is that the sentence is missing, and the switch is on your side: Settings, Siri, Excluded Apps.

Meaning three: partly on the device

The third meaning is the one to watch for in marketing. Split inference runs the first layers of a model on the phone and sends the intermediate features to a server that finishes the job. The pitch is that the raw image never leaves the device. A preprint posted on 23 October by a team at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen tested what those features still carry. On two open vision-language models and two face datasets, an honest-but-curious server, one that does its job and also looks, could recover age, gender and race from the features at every split depth tried; moving the split deeper doubled device latency and energy without reducing what leaked. A model trained to refuse biometric questions in its answers still left the attributes in the features it transmitted, and with 20 labeled examples per class the server recovered three quarters or more of what an unprotected model gave away. The authors' limits are worth repeating: faces, not food or health records; a desktop graphics card standing in for the phone; compressed features left for future work. The direction is still clear. "The image never leaves your phone" and "nothing about you leaves your phone" are different claims, and only the second is the one people hear.

Linnea's policy makes neither claim about photo recognition. Recognizing a meal in a photo is listed as a feature run by AI services from other companies. The app shows what the feature sends and to whom, nothing is sent until you agree, and what is sent is the photo you submit. Saying "the photo is sent" is a smaller promise than "on your device". It is also one we can keep.

What a policy should say

Three habits follow. Name the meaning per feature, not per app: read on the device and never uploaded; kept on the device but offered to the system assistant; or sent, in full, to a named kind of recipient. Name the readers separately: your own servers, AI providers and the platform's assistant are three different audiences, and a sentence written for one does not cover the others. And when a platform adds a reader, as Apple did on 3 November, say what you give it before you ship the integration, not after. The gap in the second section is the one we would most like our own policy to close.

Linnea's privacy policy draws its lines feature by feature. If one of them is unclear to you, tell us which.
