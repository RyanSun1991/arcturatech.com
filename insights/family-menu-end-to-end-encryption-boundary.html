A family menu needs everyone's tastes. Here is exactly what Linnea 2.8 took out of end-to-end encryption.

Linnea 2.8 reached the App Store on 27 October with a weekly meal plan, a grocery list a family can share, and a family menu that plans one table around everyone's tastes. Privacy policy 3.2 takes effect today, 28 October. It contains a sentence we would rather explain than have you discover: the tastes a family plans around, including the ones a parent sets for a child's profile, are not end-to-end encrypted. Here is why, what we did instead, and how to read a line like that in anyone's policy.

Published 28 October 2026 · 5 min read · By Arctura Technologies

What 2.8 adds

This Week's Meal Plan picks dishes from your own recipes and the Linnea dish library, for the cuisines you eat and the time you have on weekdays. You lock the meals you like and re-plan the rest. The grocery list is grouped by aisle, leaves out what you already have at home, and can be shared with your family, so whoever buys an item ticks it off for everyone. The plan is free; with Premium, AI picks among the matching dishes.

The family menu, a Premium feature, is the part this article is about. You choose who is eating at home and get one table for everyone's tastes, sized for the number of people, with the ingredients on your grocery list. A taste is a short preference such as Not spicy, Less salt or No beef. Each member sets their own. An organizer or co-manager sets them for a managed member: the profile a parent keeps for a child, or that an adult keeps for an older parent who does not use the app.

Where end-to-end encryption stops

A managed member's profile is encrypted end to end. It can be opened only by the organizer and the co-managers they choose, and the policy's security section lists end-to-end encryption for health records and managed members among the measures we rely on. We cannot read either. We said so in our 26 October article on the Children's Privacy Code, and it is still true.

A family menu cannot work that way. It is computed for everyone eating at home, so every member of the family has to be able to read every other member's tastes, including a managed member's. Policy 3.2 therefore says, in its family section, that tastes are kept with the family so that its members can plan a family menu with them, that every member can read them, and that unlike a managed member's profile they are not end-to-end encrypted. They are protected the way the rest of your account is, with encryption in transit and on your device and restricted access, but not with keys that only the organizer and co-managers hold.

We could have left that sentence out. "Encrypted end to end" would still have been true of the profile, and most readers would not have noticed that one field of it sits outside the boundary. We think the boundary is the claim. End-to-end encryption that does not say where it ends is a slogan, and the field that sits outside is the kind a stranger could read something into: a taste such as No peanuts looks like an allergy.

What we did instead of pretending

Three things, all in the policy or the release notes.

Tastes are chosen, never inferred. The policy says Linnea never infers a taste from records or medications. A No peanuts taste exists only because a person entered it. An allergy filed in a health record stays in the record, encrypted, and nothing moves from a record or a medication list into the family's tastes unless someone types it there.

A person decides who gets to say. Each member sets their own tastes. For a managed member, only the organizer or a co-manager can set one, the same people who can open the profile. The app never sets a taste on its own.

They leave when you do. When you leave a family or are removed from it, sharing in both directions stops and your tastes are deleted. When you delete your account, it is deactivated at once and deleted with its data within 24 hours, and the policy now lists your tastes, the items you marked as at home and your ticks alongside your entries, records, shared meals and recipes. It is equally plain about what does not follow you out: the items you marked as at home and the purchases you ticked stay with the family's list, because they belong to the list rather than to you.

How to read a line like this in any policy

Two days ago we offered three questions for any app that holds a child's health data. The first was whether the company can read the information, or whether it is encrypted with a key the company does not hold. Policy 3.2 is a reminder that the honest answer is rarely one word. For Linnea it is: health records and a managed member's profile, no; the tastes used for the family menu, yes, they are handled like the rest of the account. Any product that offers both end-to-end encryption and a feature several people use together has a seam like this. The question is not whether the seam exists but whether the policy names it, in a sentence a parent can find. The second question, whether any of it can reach an AI provider, is answered feature by feature inside the app: it shows what each AI feature sends and to whom, and nothing is sent until you agree.

The practical rule for a Linnea family is short. A taste is something you would say out loud at the table. If it is something you would only tell a doctor, it belongs in the record, which stays encrypted, not in a taste the whole family can read.

Policy 3.2 is short enough to read in full. If a boundary in it is still unclear to you, that is a defect we want to hear about.
