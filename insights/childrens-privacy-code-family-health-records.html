Canada now has a Children's Privacy Code. Here is what Linnea does with a child's health data.

On 20 October, the Privacy Commissioner of Canada launched the country's first Children's Privacy Code, open for comment until 20 January 2027. It is written for online services that are directed at children or likely to be used by them. Linnea is a general-audience health app, and a child under 13 cannot hold an account. But Linnea does hold children's health information, because parents keep profiles for their kids. So we read the Code as if it were about us.

Published 26 October 2026 · 5 min read · By Arctura Technologies

What the Code expects

The Code builds on PIPEDA, Canada's federal private-sector privacy law, and sets out how the Office of the Privacy Commissioner (OPC) reads its protections for anyone under 18. It is risk-based: the more sensitive the information and the likelier the harm if it were mishandled, the stronger the protection expected. Its expectations, in brief: meaningful consent calibrated to a child's evolving capacity, with a parent or guardian consenting for children under 13 and separate opt-in choices for non-essential uses of data; the highest privacy settings by default, with location, public profiles and third-party sharing off; collecting only what a feature needs and keeping it no longer than necessary; privacy information a child can understand; practical ways to correct and delete; and no profiling of children for marketing or AI-driven decisions. It also rules out deceptive design, manipulative consent flows, engagement tricks that exploit a child's developmental stage, and inferring emotional states to extract more data.

One sentence in the Code carries the whole argument: "Privacy should not be left to children or families to protect on their own." Parental controls are not a substitute for a service that is safe by construction.

Where a family health app sits

Linnea is not directed at children, and under our terms a child under 13 cannot have an account. Teenagers can, within the age limits in those terms, which also ask anyone under the age of majority to use Linnea with a parent or guardian involved. And parents keep managed profiles for younger children who do not use the app at all. Both put children's information in our hands, and the Code says an organization cannot step around it because children were not the intended audience.

The managed profile is the harder case. The child whose medications, symptoms and lab results are in it has no account, no settings and, if they are five, no way to read a notice. The Code's answer is that protection has to be built into the product rather than delegated to the parent after the fact. We agree: the design has to make the choices the child cannot make.

What Linnea does with a managed member's information

Everything below comes from Linnea's privacy policy (version 3.1, effective 23 October 2026) and the Linnea product page.

No account and nothing to discover. A managed member is a profile an organizer keeps inside their own family group for someone who does not use Linnea, such as a child or an older parent. Only the organizer and the co-managers they choose can open it. There is no public profile and no visibility setting to get wrong.

Encrypted end to end. The policy's security section lists end-to-end encryption for health records and managed members among the measures we rely on. It also says plainly that no system is completely secure, and that a lost records recovery code cannot be replaced because we do not hold the key. For a child's data that is the point: the promise not to use the information for anything else is enforced by the fact that we cannot read it.

Never to an AI provider, never to Apple Health or Health Connect. The policy's AI section lists what is never sent to a provider: raw Apple Health and Health Connect data, health records, entries for managed members, and what family members share with you. A managed member's entries are also never saved to Apple Health or Health Connect, including the organizer's own. The Code asks for third-party sharing and AI profiling to be off by default for children. For a managed member there is no switch; the path does not exist.

Notifications carry no content. Medication, supplement and water reminders show a fixed message such as "Time for your medication", never a name or dose. A care alert tells the members a person has chosen only that a scheduled medication has not been logged. Family notifications show only the kind of event, and widgets and the Lock Screen show counts and status only.

No advertising, no ad profiles, no cross-app tracking, for anyone. We do not sell personal information, use it for advertising, or track people across other companies' apps and websites. Crash reports and usage events never include values, medication names, notes or what you type, and can be turned off in the app. The Code asks for this as a default for children; we apply it to everyone.

Deletion follows the person who kept the profile. Entries and records can be deleted at any time. When an organizer deletes their account, it is deactivated at once and deleted with its data within 24 hours, and managed members whom nobody else manages are deleted with it. A child's profile does not outlive the adults who keep it.

The adult carries a responsibility too. The policy asks you to add or share information about other people only if you are allowed to, and warns that what a family member has already seen or saved cannot be recalled. We would rather say that than pretend software can revoke what someone has read.

What we do not claim

We do not describe any of this as compliance. The Code is open for comment until 20 January 2027 and may be updated, and whether an organization meets it is the regulator's judgment, not ours. Some of its expectations have no counterpart in Linnea: we do not run age assurance, because our terms require an account holder to be at least 13 and responsibility for a managed profile sits with the adult who created it; and our policy has no child-facing version, because the people who read it are adults. One idea in the Code is an open question for any product with managed profiles, ours included: evolving capacity, the principle that a child's say over their own information should grow with them. We do not have a neat answer yet.

Three questions for any app that holds a child's health data

Read as a parent, the Code reduces to three questions a privacy policy should answer in plain words. Can the company read the child's information, or is it encrypted with a key the company does not hold? Can any of it reach an AI provider, an analytics tool or an advertiser? What happens to it when the adult who created it leaves? An app that answers all three is doing the Code's work whether or not the Code applies to it.
