Photo food logging has a benchmark problem. Here is what Linnea's 22.4% is measured on, and what it is not.

Two preprints posted in October say the same thing about logging a meal from a photograph: the number does not travel. One shows that a model trained to weigh food from a single photo kept its accuracy only on the dataset it learned from, and that a common way of splitting that dataset overstates accuracy. The other treats correcting a photo result as normal use and measures what it costs. Linnea publishes a photo number of its own, a 22.4% median error. Here is what it is measured on, where the first paper's warning applies to it, and why the second paper is about the part of the product that matters more.

Published 2 November 2026 · 5 min read · By Arctura Technologies

A gram scale that stays home

The first preprint, posted on 21 October, fine-tuned an image model on 2,253 weighed dishes from Nutrition5k, a public dataset from Google Research of dishes photographed from above and weighed. On dishes from the same dataset its mean error was 24.8 grams, less than half that of a general-purpose vision-language model given no training data. On three outside datasets, with other cameras and foods, it did no better than that untrained model. Its sense of scale failed in two ways: it shifted with how much of the scene was in the frame, and it was wrong by a different factor for each food. Prediction intervals that covered the true weight 90% of the time on Nutrition5k covered it at most half the time elsewhere. A few labeled photographs from the new setting restored much of the accuracy; a larger model did not.

The finding that matters most to anyone publishing a number is about the test itself. Nutrition5k photographed many plates repeatedly as ingredients were added, so two dishes taken seconds apart can be almost identical. A test set drawn at random shared those near-twins with the training set, and the model scored better on exactly those dishes. The authors' conclusion fits in one sentence: "Single-image mass estimators therefore need labeled photographs from the setting where they are used."

What our 22.4% is measured on

Our photo figure comes from the "Linnea, measured" page: overhead photos of 60 weighed Nutrition5k dishes that were never used to tune Linnea, grouped into 2,000 random days of 4 dishes, measured on 19 October 2026. It is the median error of a day's calories; half of the days came closer. Read against the paper, three things follow. It is a Nutrition5k number, so it is the easy case: the same overhead camera and cafeteria plates, not a phone held over a bowl at a kitchen table. It is a daily number: four dishes average each other out, and a single dish is harder, as the page says. The 60 dishes were held out of tuning, but we have not checked them for near-twins of dishes we did use, the leak the paper describes; until we have, its warning applies to us too.

That is why the page lists it last, under 5.7% for a dish computed from its ingredients and weights and 9.5% for a meal described in words or by voice. A number like 22.4% is good for one thing: telling you how much checking to expect.

Correction is the product

The second preprint, posted on 30 October by a team at Emory University and the University of Virginia, starts from the observation that a photo rarely settles what a food is, what is hidden in it, how it was prepared or how much there is, so correcting the record is part of normal use rather than a failure. Working with dietitians, the authors name five recurring challenges: foods that look alike, ingredients hidden under others, mixed dishes, preparation such as added oil, and portion. They compare two ways of fixing a wrong record, with thirty people: searching a database and editing fields by hand, as in a widely used commercial app, against telling the system in words what is wrong and verifying what it regenerates. The second was rated at less than two thirds the workload of the first, took about a fifth less time, needed about half the clicks, and was preferred by 27 of the 30. The authors are careful about what that shows: everyone did the manual task first, the errors were given rather than found, and the reference records were self-reported rather than weighed.

Linnea is built on the same premise. A photo result is never a number. It is a list of dishes and ingredients to check, with calories, protein and 20 more nutrients, and it shows the portion it assumed so you can change it. Wherever it can, Linnea computes nutrition from official composition tables rather than from a model's guess, so a dish can be corrected ingredient by ingredient before it is saved. Logging a meal from a photo takes three taps, not counting the wait for recognition. What we do not have is the paper's number: how long correcting a photo result takes, and how often people change one. That measurement decides whether photo logging can be kept up, and it is the one we would most like to add to the page.

Where the photo goes

Recognizing a meal in a photo is one of the features Linnea's privacy policy lists as run by AI services from other companies. The app asks first: it shows what the feature sends and to whom, and nothing is sent until you agree. It sends the photo you submit, and does not add your name, email address or date of birth, and you can turn AI features off at any time while the rest of Linnea keeps working. A small preview of a meal photo you log is kept on your device only, encrypted, and is deleted with the entry. A photo of a nutrition label is different: it is read on your device and is not uploaded, and so is a scanned paper lab report. We would rather draw that line feature by feature than let "on your device" blur across features it does not cover.

How to read any photo-accuracy claim

Three questions settle most of it. Measured on which photographs, taken by whom, and were any of them used to train or tune the model? Per dish or per day, median or mean, and how many? And what happens after the number: can you see what the model assumed and change it before anything is saved, and how long does that take? An app that answers the first two has published a benchmark. Only an app that answers the third has published how it works.
